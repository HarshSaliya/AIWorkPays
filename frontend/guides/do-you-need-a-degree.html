Do you need a degree to get AI work?

For most of these platforms, no. Here's which ones genuinely don't care, and which ones quietly do.

For most of the platforms on this site, no. The more useful question is what actually decides your application — because that differs sharply, and it determines where your time is best spent.

The gate is usually a test, not a credential

Generalist AI-training platforms mostly screen with a written assessment. They are measuring whether you follow instructions precisely and write clearly enough for a reviewer to trust your judgement. A degree is neither asked for nor especially predictive of that, which is why these platforms don't lean on it.

If that's the gate you're facing, preparation beats credentials — see how the written assessment actually works.

Where a degree genuinely matters

The expert marketplaces are the exception. Platforms matching contributors to specialist work — medicine, law, advanced mathematics, professional engineering — use credentials as the proxy for the expertise the client is paying for. On those, an advanced degree isn't decoration; it's the product.

This is also where the highest advertised rates live, which is why those numbers are not representative of what a generalist applicant will be offered.

The middle case

Some platforms ask for education on the form without making it a hard filter. It feeds matching rather than eligibility: it may affect which projects you're offered, not whether you're accepted at all.

If you don't have a degree

  • Filter the ledger toward written-assessment and no-interview platforms.
  • Put your preparation into the assessment itself, which is the thing actually being scored.
  • If you code, say so — coding queues frequently pay several times the general rate and are tested by task, not by transcript.

The quiz weights education as one of seven factors and never eliminates a platform outright, so you can see where you stand across all of them at once.
